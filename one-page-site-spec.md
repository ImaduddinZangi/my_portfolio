# One-Page Site — Build Spec

**Purpose:** convince the owner of an operations-heavy business, in under 90 seconds, that you can build their system — and get them onto a call.

This is not a portfolio. A portfolio says "look what I made." A sales page says "here is your problem, here is proof I've solved it before, here is how we start." Every section below exists to move a stranger one step closer to booking.

---

One page. One call-to-action, repeated three times (hero, after case studies, footer). No blog, no "services" menu, no contact form that goes to an inbox you check twice a week — a booking link only.

## 4 · Case studies

This is the section that sells. Three, no more. Each follows the same shape:

> **The problem** — two sentences, in the client's language, not yours.
> **What I built** — three or four sentences.
> **The stack** — one line.
> **The result** — the number.
> **Proof** — live link, screenshots, or an embedded walkthrough video.

### Case study 1 — Optima.AI

Lead with this one. It's public, it's verifiable, and you built all of it alone.

The angle: *one developer, one commercial product, eleven business customers.* Problem: a UK health testing company needed to sell kits online, ingest lab results, and let partner organisations run their own branded storefronts. Result: 11 partner tenants on their own subdomains, ~300 kit listings, live Stripe billing, still retained for maintenance.

Proof: link to optimatesting.co.uk plus three screenshots (storefront, partner dashboard, generated report).

### Case study 2 — CDI Tech

The angle: *scale and endurance.* Fourteen months, one client, three connected products across browser, desktop and mobile, plus a legacy schema rebuilt from the ground up and geospatial routing for field engineers.

You have no live link here, so this one needs a **5-minute screen-recorded walkthrough** or a set of annotated screenshots. Without a visual, this case study is just claims.

Be straightforward that the engagement closed when the client's owner died. It answers "why can't I call them?" before anyone asks.

### Case study 3 — Mills ERP + Car Inventory (combined)

The angle: *software running real, physical, high-value operations.* Two large mills in Gujranwala on double-entry accounting and multi-warehouse inventory; eight car dealerships running vehicle inventory, ledgers and installment plans. Both white-labelled under the client's own brand. Both in daily production use.

This is your most differentiated work and the least visible. It needs screenshots or a walkthrough more than anything else on the page. Check with Labverse before publishing the mill names.

**Leave out:** MatMate until it's live on the stores, and anything internal to Africa House Pakistan without their sign-off.

## 5 · How I work

Your actual differentiator is delivery, and delivery is invisible until someone has already hired you. This section is how you make it visible beforehand. Four promises:

**Fixed scope, fixed timeline.** Agreed in writing before a line of code. You know the date and the number on day one.

**A working demo every week.** No progress reports, no black boxes — you see the thing running.

**You own everything from day one.** Repositories, cloud accounts and domains in your name, not mine.

**I finish what I start.** When I moved companies mid-project, my contract required me to see two client systems through to completion — so I did, alongside a new full-time job. One of those clients still sends me maintenance work eighteen months later.

That last one is the strongest sentence available to you. It's specific, it's checkable, and it demonstrates the exact trait a nervous buyer is scanning for.

## 6 · Ways to start

Three tiers. The first one exists to remove the risk of hiring a stranger, and it's what most people will actually buy first.

**Architecture & scoping sprint — one week, fixed fee.**
I map your operation, design the data model, and hand you an architecture document and a milestone plan with costs. Yours to keep, whether or not I build it.

**Build — four to eight weeks, fixed scope.**
Weekly demos, agreed milestones, handover documentation at the end.

**Ongoing engineering — monthly retainer.**
Capped hours, priority response, for systems already running.

The scoping sprint is the important one. "Hire an unknown developer for three months" is a terrifying decision; "pay for one week of thinking and keep the document" is easy. Land those and a real share convert into builds.

Put prices on the page if you can bear to. Prices filter out time-wasters and signal confidence. If you'd rather not, write "from £X" for the sprint only.

## 7 · About

Four sentences, first person, no corporate voice. Where you're based, who you work with, how many years, and one human detail. Include a real photo — a page selling trust with no face on it undercuts itself.

> I'm Imaduddin Zangi, a full-stack developer in Lahore. For five years I've built operations software for companies in the UK, US, Australia and the Gulf — usually as the only engineer on the project, from database design through to deployment. I work best on the unglamorous systems: ledgers, inventory, inspections, dispatch. I'm currently full-time at Africa House Pakistan and take on a limited number of outside projects.

That last clause does real work — scarcity, and honesty about your availability.

## 8 · FAQ

Six questions, each answering a real objection. Never raise credentials, degrees or gaps — introducing a doubt the reader didn't have is self-sabotage. Answer what they're actually worried about:

- **Who owns the code?** You do. Repos and cloud accounts in your name from day one.
- **What if you become unavailable?** Handover documentation with every project, and your infrastructure is yours — no lock-in.
- **How do we work across time zones?** Pakistan time overlaps the whole UK and European working day and Gulf mornings. Async by default, one fixed call each week.
- **Can we start small?** Yes — the one-week scoping sprint.
- **Do you work alongside an existing team?** Yes, including taking over legacy codebases.
- **What don't you do?** Native iOS/Android in Swift or Kotlin, machine-learning research, and design-only work. Naming what you decline makes everything else more credible.

## Visual direction — purple

One honest caveat before the palette. Your buyers are mill owners, dealership owners and inspection company operators. That's a conservative audience, and bright purple is the house colour of AI startups, crypto projects and every Tailwind landing-page template on the internet. Used carelessly it will make a serious operations engineer look like a generic SaaS site.

Used deliberately, it does the opposite — deep purple is one of the few colours that reads as *authoritative* rather than trendy, and almost nobody in your niche uses it. Your competitors are all navy blue. So purple is a genuine differentiator, provided you take it dark and restrained rather than bright and saturated.

### Palette — "Ink & Iris"

```css
:root {
  --ink:        #1B1526;  /* body text — near-black with a purple bias */
  --purple-900: #2E1F52;  /* hero + footer backgrounds */
  --purple-600: #5B3FA8;  /* buttons, links, active states */
  --purple-300: #B9A8E8;  /* accents on dark backgrounds only */
  --purple-50:  #F4F1FA;  /* tinted section backgrounds */
  --paper:      #FBFAF8;  /* page background — warm off-white, not pure white */
  --border:     #E4DFEE;
  --amber:      #C98A2E;  /* one warm accent, used sparingly */
}
```

The warm off-white matters. Purple on pure `#FFFFFF` looks cold and screen-like; on a warm paper tone it looks printed and considered. It's a small change that does a disproportionate amount of work.

The single amber accent exists to stop the page reading as monochrome — use it for one thing only, such as the underline beneath the section you most want read. Never for buttons.

### Where the colour goes

**Dark hero.** `--purple-900` background, `--paper` text, one thin `--purple-300` rule under the headline. This is the only place a light purple appears on dark.

**Light body.** Everything from the proof strip down sits on `--paper` with `--ink` text. Case studies alternate between `--paper` and `--purple-50` to create rhythm and separate them without borders.

**Buttons.** Solid `--purple-600`, white text, no gradient, no shadow, generous padding, slightly rounded corners. Hover darkens toward `--purple-900`. The CTA is the most saturated thing on the page — that's how the eye finds it.

**Links.** `--purple-600` with a visible underline.

**Dark footer.** `--purple-900` again, bookending the hero.

Roughly: 60% paper, 30% ink text, 10% purple. The dark hero and footer are the exception that makes the ratio work.

### Rules that keep it from looking templated

- **No gradients.** Purple-to-pink or purple-to-blue gradients are the single strongest signal of a template. One flat colour throughout.
- **No purple body text.** Body copy is `--ink`. Purple is for structure and emphasis only.
- **No light purple on white.** `--purple-300` exists for dark backgrounds and nothing else — it fails contrast on paper.
- **Screenshots stay neutral.** Your case-study screenshots carry their clients' own brand colours and will clash. Frame them on a plain light card with a thin `--border` and leave them completely untinted. Never overlay them with purple.
- **Check contrast.** `--purple-600` on `--paper` and `--paper` on `--purple-900` both clear WCAG AA comfortably. Anything else, test it before shipping.

### Typography

The pairing is what stops deep purple from reading as generic SaaS. Use a **serif for headlines** — Fraunces, Instrument Serif or Source Serif — against a neutral sans for body copy. Serif headlines in deep purple read editorial and grown-up; sans headlines in purple read startup.

Body copy at 18px minimum with generous line height. Your buyers are often in their fifties and sixties and will be reading on a phone. Small grey text loses you clients.

## Build

Next.js static export on Vercel, your own domain, Tailwind. It's your stack, so the site itself becomes a work sample — which means performance matters: it must load fast on a phone on mobile data, because half your visitors will open it on one.

Add Plausible or Vercel Analytics so you can see whether anyone reaches the case studies.

Resist every temptation to over-build. No CMS, no animations library, no dark-mode toggle. A fast, plain, well-written page converts better than a clever one, and a stranger judges you on the writing, not the transitions.

Realistic timeline: two days for the page, one week for the assets.
